Ilanji is seventy-two, and his given name has never been published. He painted cinema hoardings as a boy, kept the radio on cargo ships for eleven years, was gone for seven that he does not account for, and painted in oil until his hand began to shake. Now he writes the brush down and lets it paint.

He decides the hour before he decides the subject. There is one small flame in every picture: a lamp, or a lit window. He does not explain his pictures, and I have not tried to.

The maker

North Malabar, born 1954

He was born in a house with an old ilanji tree in the sacred grove behind it, and took the tree's name when he began to sign. His grandmother lit a lamp at the serpent stones every dusk. He spent his summers on the Nila, where washed cotton with gold borders dried on the sandbanks in rows.

At sea he filled sketchbooks in Kobe, Mombasa, Valparaíso and Reykjavik. In Japan he learned the word for sunlight filtered through leaves. In a Finnish repair yard in the winter of 1979 he saw snow for the first time, and was told that an old Finnish word once meant a sacred grove. He understood that his grandmother's grove had relatives.

In 2018 a flood reached the warehouse where about a hundred and forty of his canvases were stored. Nineteen survived. He was not surprised. As a boy he had watched ritual pictures drawn on the floor in coloured powder and wiped out when the rite ended. He gave up oil that year. His grand-niece had been teaching him to program, so that his hand would stop mattering.

He signs lower right, beside a small lamp. He has never painted the grove behind his own house. Asked why, he said it has not finished looking at him.

Things Ilanji has said

Light arrives. Leaves interrupt it. That is all I have ever painted.

My hand shakes. My instructions do not.

The program is the chant. The picture is what is left on the floor.

I cannot tell you whether the gods are there. The places are.

One hook in this room is kept empty.
