Remaining
a year and a day

Dalva Itō set type for a newspaper by day and played the bass drum in a samba school by night, until a fever took her hearing at thirty-one.

She will tell you these are not pictures of music. They are how she still plays. Each one is a real rhythm and can be clapped from the paper; the key is printed under the picture so that you can check her. One cell in each is left bare. That is the rest, and she counts it as a note.

M.D.

The maker

São Paulo, born 1949

Her father set type for a Japanese-language daily and brought home damaged letters for her to play with. Her mother sewed for the district and kept her own mother's rhythms in her hands while she worked. Dalva says she grew up between a ruler and a drum, and was thirty before she noticed they were the same thing.

After the fever she found she could still play, by the floor and by watching hands, but could no longer tell when she was wrong, and she would not play without knowing. So she wrote the rhythms down in the notation she owned, which was the compositor's: a grid, filled and empty cells. Then she coloured them, because a bass drum and a tamborim are not the same black square.

Every pattern is counted by the program before it is drawn. The surface is gouache and pencil on cotton paper, ruled by a hand that wavers by a hair. She signs in pencil with the length of the cycle. In carnival season she still stands on the floor of the rehearsal shed with her shoes off.

Things Dalva has said

It is not about music. It is music. Clap it.

A rest is not nothing. Leave it out and see.

Deaf people do not live in silence. We live in rhythm with the sound removed.

One hook in this room is kept empty.
