Stanzin DolkarStanding in the Room

Hanle, Ladakh, born 1992

Share: XFacebookWhatsAppLinkedInEmail

Stanzin Dolkar is the night assistant at an observatory in Ladakh, four and a half thousand metres up. Her work is to point a telescope at a patch of sky the size of a fingernail, for astronomers who are a thousand miles away. Then she walks outside, at two in the morning, and sees all of it.

These are her skies. Nothing is photographed. Every star is placed and coloured by calculation, and the dark lanes between them are made first. Her grandmother, who herded on this plateau, had names for the dark shapes and none for the lights. Look for the one small lamp in each picture. It is there to tell you how large everything else is.

Her family are Changpa herders who winter at Hanle. She took a physics degree in Jammu and came home to the telescope in 2014. She has twice been offered a research post in the south and has twice declined.

Each picture is one program. She builds a galaxy from the inside, several hundred thousand stars with their true spread of brightness and colour, cuts the dust lanes through it, lays the faint glow of the upper air over it, and then exposes it, as she says, for an eye that has been in the dark for an hour. She allows no moon and no spectacle. She signs with a star drawn by compass and the altitude in metres.

The astronomers look through a keyhole. I am the one standing in the room.
My grandmother named the dark parts. The lights were only where the dark was not.
You are not looking up at it. You are standing on a rock looking out.

Hanle, three hours, the pole keeps still

Stanzin Dolkar, 2026
Computed sky and terrain, pigment print on cotton rag
216 × 288 cm

Three hours of one night, above the monastery at Hanle. Every star has drawn the path it travelled while she watched. They all turn about one point, and the star nearest that point has hardly moved. Sailors and herders steered by it for exactly that reason.

One window is lit. Someone inside is awake, with a butter lamp, under all of this. Stand close and follow a single arc from end to end. Then step back and let the whole wheel turn.

Go Sun-deokThe Paper Is the Air

Hado-ri, Jeju, born 1944

Share: XFacebookWhatsAppLinkedInEmail

Go Sun-deok dived for abalone off the island of Jeju for sixty-one years, on a single breath at a time. For fifty of those years she painted in ink on winter evenings, on the backs of the cooperative's ledgers, and showed nobody.

Photographs of the diving women show the women. She paints what the women saw. Each sheet is one dive, with a way down and a way up. There is more bare paper than ink, because the paper is the air, and a diver does not spend air. The only colour is her seal. It reads breath, and it sits where her float would be.

Her mother dived, and her grandmother. At fifteen she went out with the women. A diver's whole skill, she says, is knowing to the second how much breath is left, and never taking the one last shell that would cost it.

The cold finished her wrists in 2022. Her granddaughter, who builds models of the ocean at the university, sat with her through a winter and wrote down how she paints: how wet, how fast, where the brush lifts, how long to wait. Sun-deok does not touch the machine. She says what is wrong with the picture, and the numbers are changed. There is no white paint and no correction. A sheet that is wrong is begun again.

You do not look at the sea. You are in it or you are not.
The paper is the air. Do not spend it.
My hands are finished. My eyes went down for sixty years. Those still work.

Udo channel, kelp, the current turning

Go Sun-deok, 2026
Ink on mulberry paper, laid by program
136 × 240 cm

Look at the kelp. Low down it leans one way. Higher up it leans the other. That is how a diver knows the current has turned, a moment before she feels it on her body, and it is the moment to decide whether to go on or go up.

Every stalk is a single stroke, made from the root to the tip without lifting. She told her granddaughter that if a stroke stops to think, the kelp dies.

Ruhi VaghelaBuilt Downward

Patan, Gujarat, born 1985

Share: XFacebookWhatsAppLinkedInEmail

For fifteen years Ruhi Vaghela measured the stepwells of Gujarat and Rajasthan for the state, stair by stair, first with a tape and a plumb line and later with a laser. A cathedral goes up. These buildings go down, as far as thirty metres, to reach water.

She builds each well again from its measurements and puts the sun where the sun was. For a few minutes around noon, in May, one shaft of it reaches the bottom. Everything else you see is stone lit by other stone. There is one person in each picture, and she is carrying water. You will find her late, and when you do, the building will get larger.

She grew up five minutes from one of the great stepwells and played in it before it had a fence. Her father cut sandstone for temple repairs. He told her that the masons carved the lowest storey as finely as the highest, although it would lie under water for most of the year and be seen by no one.

She measured about sixty wells. Each picture is a single program: the well as a solid model, stairs and pillars repeated as the masons repeated them, hard sun, soft sky, and the light that bounces between. She finishes them matte, like lime plaster. She signs with a mason's mark and the depth in metres, and the depth is true.

A temple goes up to show you the god. A well goes down to show you the water. I know which I needed more.
They carved the bottom storey for the fish.
Count the steps. That is the only way to believe it.

Halina Mróz QuispeEverything Is in Order

Antofagasta, born 1961

Share: XFacebookWhatsAppLinkedInEmail

Halina Mróz Quispe inspected shipping containers in a Chilean port, at night, for thirty-one years. Her work was to compare what a paper said was in the box with what was in the box.

Each of these pictures is a declaration, and you are the inspector. Every room is built as a measured solid and lit by calculation, and everything in it is correct to the millimetre, except for one item. It is not hidden. I will not tell you which. Somewhere in each picture hangs a plumb line. It is always true, and it is there so that you know what to believe.

Her father was a ship's engineer from Gdynia who walked off a freighter in Antofagasta and never explained it. Her mother came down from a village near the Bolivian border. He taught her to draw machines in section. She taught her that the mountains were persons, and that you greet them.

Before the port she worked two seasons for a survey crew on the salt flats, holding the staff and the plumb bob. She wrote her own programs, because the ones she could buy would not let her break one law of nature and keep all the others. Each picture is stamped lower left, a few degrees off level, with her initials and an hour that no clock shows.

Everything is in order. Please check again.
I do not invent. I declare one item incorrectly.
People ask what the room means. It is 6.2 metres by 9. The sun was at forty degrees.

Tobi AdeyemiEleven Seconds

Lagos, born 1999

Share: XFacebookWhatsAppLinkedInEmail

Tobi Adeyemi is a motorcycle dispatch rider in Lagos. He was three years into an architecture degree when the fees stopped, and he has been on the bike ever since.

To get through a Lagos junction alive, a rider looks ahead, at both mirrors, down at the road and up at the bridge, and puts it all together in a few seconds. That is how he paints. Each piece of a picture is one of those looks, and each title tells you how many seconds the whole thing took. Somewhere in everything he makes there is a piece of bus yellow with two black stripes.

He was raised in Yaba by his mother, who sells cloth in Balogun market. At university he was good at the thing first-years find hardest: holding a plan, a section and an elevation in the head and knowing they are one object. Riding in Lagos turned out to be the same exercise at speed, with a hand's width on either side.

He went down once, at Ojuelegba in 2022, and broke a collarbone. In the six weeks off the bike he began to draw what he had seen in the seconds before. He builds each junction as a rough model, takes his views through it, and cuts the panel along lines that are already there: a kerb, a wire, the edge of a bus. He signs with his rider number, 417.

Lagos no get one side. Why my picture go get one side?
Mirror is not the past. Mirror is behind you, now.
I no dey paint fast. I dey paint wetin fast.

IlanjiThe Hour Before the Subject

North Malabar, born 1954

Share: XFacebookWhatsAppLinkedInEmail

Ilanji is seventy-two, and his given name has never been published. He painted cinema hoardings as a boy, kept the radio on cargo ships for eleven years, and painted in oil until his hand began to shake. Now he writes the brush down, stroke by stroke, and lets it paint.

He decides the hour before he decides the subject. A grove at lamp-lighting. A river at noon. Snow at three in the afternoon. In nearly every picture there is one small flame, a lamp or a lit window, and the whole painting is built outward from it. I have hung the room dark, so that you can find them.

He was born in a house with an old ilanji tree in the sacred grove behind it, and took the tree's name when he began to sign. His grandmother lit a lamp at the serpent stones every dusk. At sea he filled sketchbooks in Kobe, Mombasa, Valparaíso and Reykjavik.

In 2018 a flood reached the warehouse where about a hundred and forty of his canvases were stored. Nineteen survived. He was not surprised. As a boy he had watched ritual pictures drawn on the floor in coloured powder and wiped away when the rite ended. He gave up oil that year. Each picture now is a place built in three dimensions, lit, and then painted by a brush he has described in every particular. He has never painted the grove behind his own house. Asked why, he said it has not finished looking at him.

Light arrives. Leaves interrupt it. That is all I have ever painted.
My hand shakes. My instructions do not.
The program is the chant. The picture is what is left on the floor.
I cannot tell you whether the gods are there. The places are.
